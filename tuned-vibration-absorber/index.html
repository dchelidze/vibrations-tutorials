Tuned vibration absorbers, Den Hartog fixed points, and vibration isolation

Attaching a small secondary oscillator $(m_a, k_a, c_a)$ to a resonating primary structure creates an antiresonance force-cancellation notch at $\omega = \omega_a$. We visualize Den Hartog's invariant fixed points $P$ and $Q$, the mass-ratio vs. absorber-stroke trade-off, and how passive vibration isolation crosses $T = 1$ at $r = \sqrt{2}$.

Part 1 · Dynamic absorber mechanics & phasor cancellation

1. An undamped absorber zeroes primary motion at its tuning frequency by opposing the drive force

In steady state, the absorber exerts a reaction force $F_{a\to 1} = \omega^2 m_a X_a$ back onto the primary mass $m_1$. When $\zeta_a = 0$ and the drive hits the absorber's standalone natural frequency $r = \beta$ ($\omega = \omega_a = \sqrt{k_a/m_a}$), the absorber swings $180^\circ$ out of phase with $F_0$ so that $F_{a\to 1} = -F_0$ identically—leaving zero net force on $m_1$ and freezing $X_1 = 0$.

Part 2 · Den Hartog's fixed-point theorem

2. Sweeping absorber damping pivots every FRF curve through two invariant fixed points $P$ and $Q$

Adding a damper $c_a$ across the absorber spring eliminates the infinite split peaks, turning the absorber into a broadband tuned mass damper (TMD). Remarkably, for an undamped primary mass, the primary FRF magnitude passes through two exact frequencies $r_P$ and $r_Q$ whose heights are strictly independent of $\zeta_a$.

$$\left|\frac{X_1}{F_0/k_1}\right| = \sqrt{\frac{(\beta^2 - r^2)^2 + (2\beta\zeta_a r)^2}{\big[(1 - r^2)(\beta^2 - r^2) - \mu\beta^2 r^2\big]^2 + (2\beta\zeta_a r)^2\big[1 - r^2(1+\mu)\big]^2}}$$

Optional advanced note: Why fixed points break when primary damping $\zeta_1 > 0$

The fixed-point identity relies on the numerator and denominator containing $\zeta_a^2$ in a single linear-fractional ratio $(A^2 + \zeta_a^2 B^2)/(C^2 + \zeta_a^2 D^2)$, which requires zero primary damping ($\zeta_1 = 0$). When the primary structure itself has light damping ($\zeta_1 \in [0.01, 0.05]$), the cross-term $4\zeta_1\zeta_a$ slightly blurs the exact intersections $P$ and $Q$, and the numerical minimax optimum shifts $\beta_{\mathrm{opt}}$ slightly below $1/(1+\mu)$ while reducing the peak height below $\sqrt{1 + 2/\mu}$. Den Hartog's closed form remains the standard starting point for design.

Part 3 · Engineering trade-offs: mass ratio vs. stroke clearance

3. Larger mass ratio widens the notch and lowers peak amplification at the cost of added mass

Suppressing primary motion transfers vibrational energy into the absorber mass. At the undamped notch $r = \beta$, the absorber must swing with amplitude $|X_a| = F_0/k_a = X_{\mathrm{st}}/(\mu\beta^2)$: making $\mu$ very small looks attractive for weight, but demands huge relative clearance $|X_a - X_1|$ and yields a razor-thin frequency split between $r_1$ and $r_2$.

Part 4 · Passive vibration isolation & transmissibility

4. Passive isolation works only above $r = \sqrt{2}$, where all damping curves cross $T = 1$

Instead of attaching a secondary absorber mass, passive vibration isolation places a compliant mount $(k, c)$ beneath the machine or payload. Both force transmissibility $|F_T|/F_0$ and base-excitation displacement transmissibility $|X|/Y$ obey the identical curve $T(r,\zeta)$. Every damping curve crosses $(r, T) = (\sqrt{2}, 1)$ identically; above $r = \sqrt{2}$, adding viscous damping actually increases transmitted force because the damper transmits $\mathrm{i}c\omega X$ straight into the foundation.

$$T(r, \zeta) = \left|\frac{F_T}{F_0}\right| = \left|\frac{X}{Y}\right| = \sqrt{\frac{1 + (2\zeta r)^2}{(1 - r^2)^2 + (2\zeta r)^2}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad T(\sqrt{2}, \zeta) \equiv 1 \;\;\forall\;\zeta \ge 0$$

Part 6 · Design workflow & verification

6. Step-by-step absorber and isolator design workflow

Design sequence (Inman Ch. 5)

  1. 1
    Identify excitation spectrum. Single constant frequency $\Rightarrow$ undamped or lightly damped TVA; variable/broadband near $\omega_1$ $\Rightarrow$ Den Hartog TMD; high-frequency drive $\Rightarrow$ passive isolator.
  2. 2
    Choose mass ratio $\mu = m_a/m_1$ from allowable peak $M_{\mathrm{max}}$. Invert $M_{\mathrm{opt}} = \sqrt{1 + 2/\mu}$ to get $\mu \ge 2 / (M_{\mathrm{max}}^2 - 1)$ (typically $0.05 \le \mu \le 0.25$).
  3. 3
    Compute Den Hartog stiffness and damping. Set $\omega_a = \omega_1/(1+\mu)$, $k_a = m_a\omega_a^2$, and $c_a = 2 m_a \omega_a \sqrt{3\mu / (8(1+\mu))}$.
  4. 4
    Audit absorber relative stroke $|X_a - X_1|$. Confirm the absorber spring can accommodate its peak excursion without bottoming out or yielding.
  5. 5
    For isolators, balance sag vs. startup resonance. Pick $\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \le \omega_{\mathrm{op}}/\sqrt{2}$ checking static deflection $\delta_{\mathrm{st}} = g/\omega_{\mathrm{n}}^2$, and use modest $\zeta$ to control startup without ruining high-$r$ roll-off.
