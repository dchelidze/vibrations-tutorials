Fourier and Laplace transforms in vibration analysis

A single-degree-of-freedom damped oscillator connects three equivalent descriptions of linear dynamics: the time-domain impulse response function $h(t)$, the complex-plane Laplace transfer function $H(s)$, and the steady-state Fourier frequency response function $H(\mathrm{i}\omega)$. We show geometrically how the Fourier transform emerges as the imaginary-axis slice $s = \mathrm{i}\omega$ of the Laplace surface, why convergence depends on pole locations, and how differentiation and reciprocals produce the six standard dimensional FRFs used in vibration testing.

Part 1 · Physical 1-DOF system

1. One damped oscillator links time, Laplace, and frequency descriptions

For a linear mass-spring-damper system excited by an external force $f(t)$, Newton’s second law gives a second-order constant-coefficient differential equation. Compare the impulse response $h(t)$, harmonic forced response, step response, and free release below while adjusting $m$, $k$, and $\zeta$.

Governing equation and transform triad

$$m\ddot{x}(t) + c\dot{x}(t) + kx(t) = f(t)$$

Linear limit: valid for constant mass, linear spring stiffness $k$, and linear viscous damping $c$. Nonlinear restoring forces introduce amplitude-dependent natural frequencies (backbone curves) and break superposition, so a single amplitude-independent FRF no longer describes the system.

Part 2 · Complex $s$-plane geometry

2. The Fourier transform is the imaginary-axis slice of the Laplace transform

Write the complex frequency as $s = \sigma + \mathrm{i}\omega$. Because $\mathrm{e}^{-st} = \mathrm{e}^{-\sigma t}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega t}$, the unilateral Laplace transform is the Fourier transform of the exponentially weighted causal signal $x(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma t}u(t)$. The real decay rate $\sigma$ provides a convergence factor that handles undamped or growing responses.

$$\mathcal{L}\{h(t)\}(s) = \int_{0^-}^{\infty} h(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{-(\sigma + \mathrm{i}\omega)t}\,\mathrm{d}t = \mathcal{F}\!\left\{h(t)\mathrm{e}^{-\sigma t}u(t)\right\}(\omega) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad H(\mathrm{i}\omega) = \left.H(s)\right|_{s = \mathrm{i}\omega}\;\text{when }\sigma = 0 \in \mathrm{ROC}$$

Part 3 · Convolution theorem

3. Time-domain convolution equals multiplication in the transform domain

Any causal force history $f(t)$ is a superposition of delayed impulses, so the zero-initial-condition response is the Duhamel convolution integral of $f(t)$ with the impulse response $h(t)$. Transforming turns that integral into pointwise algebraic multiplication by the transfer function $H(s)$ or FRF $H(\mathrm{i}\omega)$.

$$x(t) = (h * f)(t) = \int_{0}^{t} h(t - \tau)f(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\tau \quad\xleftrightarrow{\;\mathcal{L}\;}\quad X(s) = H(s)F(s) \quad\xleftrightarrow{\;\mathcal{F}\;}\quad X(\mathrm{i}\omega) = H(\mathrm{i}\omega)F(\mathrm{i}\omega)$$

Multiplying $|F(\mathrm{i}\omega)|$ by the resonant FRF $|H(\mathrm{i}\omega)|$ amplifies the component near $\omega_{\mathrm{n}}$ and strongly attenuates the higher-frequency tone—the exact frequency-domain counterpart of the time-domain convolution integral.
Part 4 · Reading the FRF

4. Reading stiffness, damping, and mass regions from the FRF

Evaluating $H(s)$ along $s = \mathrm{i}\omega$ yields the complex receptance $\alpha(\mathrm{i}\omega) = \operatorname{Re}(\alpha) + \mathrm{i}\operatorname{Im}(\alpha) = |\alpha|\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}$. Drag the copper frequency probe directly on the Bode plot to track the response across the stiffness-controlled ($\omega \ll \omega_{\mathrm{n}}$), damping-controlled ($\omega \approx \omega_{\mathrm{n}}$), and mass-controlled ($\omega \gg \omega_{\mathrm{n}}$) regimes.

Part 5 · Dimensionality & physical sensors

5. Six frequency response functions: differentiation, reciprocals, and units

In the Fourier domain, time differentiation multiplies by $\mathrm{i}\omega$: velocity is $V(\mathrm{i}\omega) = \mathrm{i}\omega X(\mathrm{i}\omega)$ and acceleration is $\ddot{X}(\mathrm{i}\omega) = -\omega^2 X(\mathrm{i}\omega)$. Each factor of $\mathrm{i}\omega$ rotates the log-log magnitude slope by $+20\;\text{dB/dec}$, shifts phase by $+90^\circ$, and changes physical units. Inverting response-over-force ratios gives the three reciprocal impedance functions.

$$\alpha(\mathrm{i}\omega) = \frac{X}{F} \;\xrightarrow{\;\times\,\mathrm{i}\omega\;}\; Y(\mathrm{i}\omega) = \frac{V}{F} \;\xrightarrow{\;\times\,\mathrm{i}\omega\;}\; A(\mathrm{i}\omega) = \frac{\ddot{X}}{F}, \qquad Z_k = \frac{1}{\alpha},\quad Z = \frac{1}{Y},\quad M_{\mathrm{app}} = \frac{1}{A}$$
FRF name (click to focus) Definition Exact 1-DOF expression SI units Low / high-$\omega$ slopes Value at probe $\omega$
Dimensional FRFs vs. dimensionless ratios. The six functions above carry physical dimensions determined by the sensor and force transducer (e.g., an impact-hammer test with a piezoelectric accelerometer directly measures accelerance $A(\mathrm{i}\omega)$ in $\text{m/(s}^2\cdot\text{N)} = \text{kg}^{-1}$). Multiplying receptance by $k$ produces the dimensionless dynamic amplification factor $D(\mathrm{i}\omega) = k\alpha(\mathrm{i}\omega)$. By contrast, base-excitation displacement transmissibility and force transmissibility share the dimensionless transfer function $T(\mathrm{i}\omega) = \frac{1 + \mathrm{i}2\zeta r}{(1 - r^2) + \mathrm{i}2\zeta r}$, whose numerator zero causes all damping curves to cross $|T| = 1$ at $r = \sqrt{2}$ (the isolation threshold).
Part 6 · Engineering workflow & verification

6. Step-by-step vibration workflow and reproducible code

Follow the five steps below to move cleanly between time, Laplace, and frequency descriptions, or copy the live MATLAB and Python scripts populated with your current system parameters.

Analytical & experimental checklist

  1. 1
    Form the Laplace transfer function. Take the unilateral Laplace transform of $m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = f(t)$ to separate the zero-state transfer function $H(s) = (ms^2 + cs + k)^{-1}$ from the initial-condition transient terms.
  2. 2
    Inspect poles and the ROC. Compute $p_{1,2} = -\zeta\omega_{\mathrm{n}} \pm \mathrm{i}\omega_{\mathrm{d}}$. Confirm $\operatorname{Re}(p_{1,2}) < 0$ so the imaginary axis $s = \mathrm{i}\omega$ lies inside the ROC.
  3. 3
    Restrict to $s = \mathrm{i}\omega$ for steady-state FRFs. Evaluate $\alpha(\mathrm{i}\omega) = H(\mathrm{i}\omega)$ to obtain Bode magnitude/phase, quadrature peaks, and steady-state harmonic response $X(\mathrm{i}\omega) = \alpha(\mathrm{i}\omega)F(\mathrm{i}\omega)$.
  4. 4
    Match the FRF type to your sensor. Accelerometers yield $A(\mathrm{i}\omega)$, vibrometers yield $Y(\mathrm{i}\omega)$, and displacement probes yield $\alpha(\mathrm{i}\omega)$. Convert between them using factors of $\mathrm{i}\omega$ and track SI units explicitly.
  5. 5
    Identify modal parameters. Read $\omega_{\mathrm{n}}$ from the $\operatorname{Re}(\alpha) = 0$ crossing or mobility peak, extract $\zeta$ from the $-3\;\text{dB}$ half-power bandwidth or mobility Nyquist circle diameter $1/c$, and verify asymptotes ($1/k$ and $1/m$).
