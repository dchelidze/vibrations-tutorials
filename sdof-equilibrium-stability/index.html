Equilibrium stability, eigenvalues, and phase portraits of 1-DOF systems

Near an equilibrium position, a one-degree-of-freedom mechanical system reduces to a two-dimensional linear flow $\dot{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}$ governed entirely by two matrix invariants: the trace $\tau = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}) = -c/m$ and the determinant $\Delta = \det(\mathbf{A}) = k/m$. We show how the characteristic values $\lambda_{1,2}$ and eigenvectors determine every possible phase portrait, why $(\tau, \Delta)$ organizes all stability transitions geometrically, and where linearization succeeds or fails in nonlinear mechanical systems.

Part 1 · Linearization & mechanical meaning

1. Trace and determinant encode dissipation and potential curvature

Linearizing a 1-DOF mechanical system $m\ddot{q} + g(q,\dot{q}) = 0$ about an equilibrium $(q^*, 0)$ gives $m\ddot{x} + c\dot{x} + kx = 0$, where $x = q - q^*$ is the small perturbation, $k = \partial g/\partial q = V''(q^*)$ is the local effective stiffness, and $c = \partial g/\partial \dot{q}$ is the effective damping coefficient. Unlike a passive classroom spring, $k$ and $c$ can be positive, zero, or negative (inverted pendula, buckled beams, or self-excited flutter).

Drag the bead horizontally to perturb the equilibrium

First-order companion state-space form

$$\ddot{x} - \tau\dot{x} + \Delta x = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \begin{bmatrix}\dot{x} \\ \dot{v}\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 1 \\ -\Delta & \tau\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}x \\ v\end{bmatrix}$$

Mechanical reading: $\Delta = k/m = V''(q^*)/m$ measures the local curvature of the potential energy $V(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta x^2$. The trace $\tau = -c/m$ sets the energy rate $\dot{E} = \tau v^2$: negative $\tau$ removes energy, $\tau = 0$ conserves it, and positive $\tau$ injects it.

Part 2 · Characteristic equation & eigenvalue problem

2. Characteristic values set growth rates; eigenvectors set invariant directions

Seeking straight-line exponential solutions $\mathbf{z}(t) = \mathbf{v}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}$ converts $\dot{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}$ into the algebraic eigenvalue problem $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$. Nontrivial solutions exist if and only if $\lambda$ satisfies the quadratic characteristic equation $\det(\mathbf{A} - \lambda\mathbf{I}) = 0$.

$$\det(\mathbf{A} - \lambda\mathbf{I}) = \lambda^2 - \tau\lambda + \Delta = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda_{1,2} = \frac{\tau \pm \sqrt{\tau^2 - 4\Delta}}{2}, \qquad \mathbf{v}_i = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ \lambda_i\end{bmatrix}$$

Repeated roots on $\Delta = \tau^2/4$: Star (proper) node vs. improper (Jordan) node

When $D = \tau^2 - 4\Delta = 0$ and $\tau \ne 0$, the characteristic equation has a repeated nonzero real root $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = \lambda = \tau/2$ (algebraic multiplicity $m_{\mathrm{a}} = 2$). The pair $(\tau, \Delta)$ alone does not distinguish the two possible phase portraits on the parabola — the rank of $\mathbf{A} - \lambda\mathbf{I}$ (equivalently, geometric multiplicity $m_{\mathrm{g}} = 2 - \operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A} - \lambda\mathbf{I})$) decides:

Star (proper / dicritical) node: $m_{\mathrm{g}} = 2$

Exact conditions: $\Delta = \tau^2/4 > 0$ and $\mathbf{A} = \lambda\mathbf{I} = \begin{bmatrix}\lambda & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda\end{bmatrix}$ ($\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A} - \lambda\mathbf{I}) = 0$). Stable star if $\tau < 0$ ($\lambda < 0$); unstable star if $\tau > 0$ ($\lambda > 0$). Every nonzero vector is an eigenvector and $\mathbf{z}(t) = \mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}\mathbf{z}_0$ moves strictly along straight radial rays.

Improper (degenerate / Jordan) node: $m_{\mathrm{g}} = 1$

Exact conditions: $\Delta = \tau^2/4 > 0$ and $\mathbf{A} \ne \lambda\mathbf{I}$ ($\operatorname{rank}(\mathbf{A} - \lambda\mathbf{I}) = 1$). Every critically damped 1-DOF mechanical oscillator falls here because $\dot{x} = v$ fixes $A_{12} = 1 \ne 0$, leaving one eigendirection $\mathbf{v}_1 = [1, \lambda]^{\mathsf{T}}$ and secular shear $t\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}$.

Part 3 · The $(\tau, \Delta)$ stability map

3. Every qualitative change happens across three curves in the $(\tau, \Delta)$ plane

Because $\lambda_{1,2}$ depend only on $\tau$ and $\Delta$, all 1-DOF linear phase portraits live on one two-dimensional map. Drag the copper handle anywhere on the $(\tau, \Delta)$ plane below, or run a one-parameter sweep to watch the phase portrait morph across the parabola $\Delta = \tau^2/4$, the center boundary $\tau = 0$, and the static buckling line $\Delta = 0$.

Stable spiral
1. Crossing $\Delta = 0$ (static loss of stability)

One real eigenvalue passes through $\lambda = 0$. A potential well flattens and inverts into a hilltop, turning a node into a saddle — the linear signature of static buckling (pitchfork or saddle-node bifurcation).

2. Crossing $\tau = 0$ with $\Delta > 0$ (dynamic loss of stability)

A complex-conjugate pair crosses the imaginary axis at $\pm\mathrm{i}\sqrt{\Delta}$. Damping reverses sign from dissipation to energy injection — the linear mechanism behind aeroelastic flutter and Hopf bifurcation.

3. Crossing $\Delta = \tau^2/4$ (oscillatory transition)

Two real roots collide at $\lambda = \tau/2$ and split into a complex-conjugate pair. Stability does not change, but the two real eigendirections vanish and trajectories switch from non-oscillatory nodes to spirals.

4. Phase-space area, coordinate straightening, and why trajectories bend

Algebraic formulas tell us when trajectories decay; geometry shows us how the entire phase plane deforms as a fluid. Trace = phase-area divergence

The vector field divergence is $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}) = \tau$. By Liouville’s formula, every phase patch scales in area strictly as $S(t) = S(0)\mathrm{e}^{\tau t}$ — even on a conservative saddle ($\tau = 0, \Delta < 0$), stretching along $E^{\mathrm{u}}$ is exactly balanced by squeezing along $E^{\mathrm{s}}$.

Eigenvector slope equals $\lambda_i$

Because $\dot{x} = v$, any straight invariant trajectory $v(t) = m x(t)$ satisfies $\dot{x} = m x \implies x(t) = x_0\mathrm{e}^{mt}$. Therefore the slope $m$ of an invariant line in the $(x, v)$ phase plane must equal its eigenvalue $\lambda_i$: steeper lines correspond to faster rates.

Why nodes hug the slow axis

In eigen-coordinates $(y_1, y_2)$, $y_1(t) = y_{10}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_1 t}$ and $y_2(t) = y_{20}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_2 t}$, eliminating $t$ gives $y_2 = C|y_1|^{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}$. When $|\lambda_2| > |\lambda_1|$, the exponent exceeds $1$, forcing all curves to arrive tangent to the slow $y_1$-axis.

Why spirals rotate clockwise

In the upper half-plane ($v > 0$), $\dot{x} = v > 0$ pushes every trajectory strictly to the right; in the lower half-plane ($v < 0$), $\dot{x} < 0$ pushes it strictly to the left. Thus every 1-DOF mechanical spiral or center in $(x, \dot{x})$ coordinates must circulate clockwise.

Advanced geometric note: Abel–Jacobi–Liouville identity and the circle of roots

For any linear flow $\dot{\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}$, the state transition matrix $\mathbf{\Phi}(t) = \mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}t}$ maps an infinitesimal area element $\mathrm{d}x_0\,\mathrm{d}v_0$ to $\det(\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}t})\,\mathrm{d}x_0\,\mathrm{d}v_0$. Using Jacobi’s identity $\det(\mathrm{e}^{\mathbf{A}t}) = \mathrm{e}^{\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A})t} = \mathrm{e}^{\tau t}$, we obtain $S(t) = S(0)\mathrm{e}^{\tau t}$ for any measurable region $S(0)$ in the phase plane.

In the complex $\lambda$-plane (Section 2), hold stiffness $\Delta > 0$ fixed and vary damping $\tau$. While $|\tau| < 2\sqrt{\Delta}$, the complex roots satisfy $|\lambda|^2 = \lambda_1\lambda_2 = \Delta$, so $\lambda_{1,2}$ travel along the exact circle of radius $\sqrt{\Delta}$ centered at the origin, meet on the real axis at $\pm\sqrt{\Delta}$ when $|\tau| = 2\sqrt{\Delta}$, and then split along the real axis as reciprocal partners $\lambda_1\lambda_2 = \Delta$.

Part 5 · Nonlinear 1-DOF bridge & limits of linearization

5. Linearization predicts hyperbolic equilibria locally, but fails at non-hyperbolic centers

Real mechanical systems can possess multiple equilibria simultaneously — potential wells alongside inverted hilltops — and nonlinear restoring or damping terms. Advanced geometric note: Hartman–Grobman theorem and Lyapunov’s direct method

An equilibrium $\mathbf{z}^*$ is hyperbolic when no eigenvalue of the Jacobian $\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{z}^*)$ lies on the imaginary axis ($\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) \ne 0$). The Hartman–Grobman theorem proves that near a hyperbolic equilibrium, the nonlinear flow is locally topologically conjugate (smoothly deformable) to its linearized flow $\dot{\delta\mathbf{z}} = \mathbf{J}(\mathbf{z}^*)\delta\mathbf{z}$.

When $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) = 0$ — specifically at a linear center ($\tau = 0, \Delta > 0$) or a zero-eigenvalue bifurcation ($\Delta = 0$) — the linearization sits on a stability boundary and is structurally unstable. Arbitrarily small nonlinear terms determine true stability, as seen in the Non-hyperbolic center test ($\ddot{x} + \beta x^2\dot{x} + x = 0$). There, the quadratic Lyapunov candidate $E(x, v) = \tfrac{1}{2}(x^2 + v^2)$ satisfies $\dot{E} = -\beta x^2 v^2$, proving asymptotic stability for $\beta > 0$ and instability for $\beta < 0$ even though $\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{0})$ has purely imaginary eigenvalues $\pm\mathrm{i}$ for every $\beta$.

Step-by-step stability analysis workflow

  1. 1
    Locate equilibria. Write the 1-DOF system in state space $\dot{x} = v$, $\dot{v} = f(x, v)$ and solve $v^* = 0$, $f(x^*, 0) = 0$ for each equilibrium $x^*$.
  2. 2
    Evaluate the Jacobian $\mathbf{J}(x^*)$. Compute $\Delta = -\partial f/\partial x|_{x^*,0} = V''(x^*)/m$ and $\tau = \partial f/\partial v|_{x^*,0} = -c_{\text{eff}}/m$.
  3. 3
    Locate $(\tau, \Delta)$ on the Poincaré plane. Check the signs of $\Delta$, $\tau$, and $D = \tau^2 - 4\Delta$ to identify the stability type before solving any differential equation.
  4. 4
    Extract eigenvalues and eigendirections. Compute $\lambda_{1,2}$ and plot $\mathbf{v}_i = [1, \lambda_i]^{\mathsf{T}}$ plus nullclines to sketch the local phase portrait accurately.
  5. 5
    Verify hyperbolicity. If $\operatorname{Re}(\lambda_i) \ne 0$, Hartman–Grobman certifies local topological equivalence. If $\tau = 0$ or $\Delta = 0$, use energy conservation or a nonlinear Lyapunov function to determine true stability.
