Periodic forcing, Fourier epicycles, and subharmonic resonance

Any periodic excitation $f(t)$ decomposes into a discrete ladder of harmonic lines at $n\omega_0$. By superposition, a linear SDOF oscillator multiplies each line by its complex transfer function $H(\mathrm{i}nr_0)$—causing dramatic subharmonic resonance whenever a higher harmonic lands on $n r_0 = 1$, even when the drive itself repeats at a fraction of $\omega_{\mathrm{n}}$.

Part 1 · Rotating Fourier epicycles

1. Tip-to-tail rotating harmonics synthesize the filtered periodic steady state

Each harmonic $n$ rotates at angular speed $n\omega_0$ with radius $c_n |H(\mathrm{i}nr_0)|$ and phase lag $\phi_n$. When $n r_0 \approx 1$ for a nonzero input harmonic, that single circle inflates and dominates the epicycle chain—driving the mass at $n$ cycles per input period.

Part 2 · Mechanical frequency-domain filtering

2. The SDOF FRF $|H(\mathrm{i}r)|$ acts as a mechanical bandpass selector on discrete input lines

In the frequency domain, periodic forcing is a comb of discrete lines spaced by $r_0$. Multiplying each line by the continuous SDOF receptance $|H(\mathrm{i}r)|$ produces the output bar chart on the right: whichever nonzero harmonic falls nearest to $r = 1$ receives amplification $\approx 1/(2\zeta)$, while high harmonics are crushed by the $1/r^2$ tail.

Part 3 · Subharmonic resonance landscape

3. Sweeping $r_0$ uncovers a forest of subharmonic peaks at $r_0 = 1/n$

Plotting total output RMS $X_{\mathrm{rms}}(r_0)$ reveals why non-sinusoidal periodic machinery can resonate at fractions of its natural frequency—and why waveform symmetry matters: a square wave has peaks only at odd reciprocals ($1/5, 1/3, 1$), a half-rectified pulse skips $1/3$ while lighting up $1/2$, and a sawtooth lights up every integer reciprocal $1/n$.
